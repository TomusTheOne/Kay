The underworld

Cenotes were never just swimming holes. They were the door.

In the Popol Vuh, Xibalba is the place beneath — reached through caves and through water. Every cenote in the Yucatán was a mouth of the earth: somewhere to leave an offering, somewhere you did not swim for fun.

The limestone is still doing what it has always done. Rain falls, dissolves the rock, and drains into one of the largest flooded cave systems on the planet. We take you a respectful distance inside it — on a guideline, within daylight, four divers at a time.

Salt water

The second largest reef on earth starts here.

The Mesoamerican Barrier Reef runs from Isla Contoy to Honduras and passes within a kilometre of Tulum beach. Twenty minutes in the boat, two tanks, turtles more often than not.

  • Green and loggerhead turtles on the seagrass beds year-round.
  • Eagle rays and nurse sharks over the sand channels.
  • Reef-safe sunscreen only — we will hand you some if you forget.
  • Bull sharks off Playa del Carmen, November to March.

Before you come

Questions
worth asking.

Do I need to be a certified diver to dive a cenote?

For cavern diving, yes — Open Water is the minimum, and for Angelita or The Pit you need Advanced. If you are not certified we run guided cenote snorkelling and Discover Scuba sessions in the open, daylight sections, which are genuinely beautiful in their own right.

What is the difference between cavern diving and cave diving?

Cavern diving stays inside the natural daylight zone — within roughly 60 metres of the entrance, no deeper than 21 metres, always on a permanent guideline, always with a direct route to the surface. Cave diving goes beyond daylight and needs a dedicated cave certification. Everything we guide is cavern.

How cold is the water?

Around 24–25 °C in the cenotes, every day of the year, and 26–29 °C on the reef. A 3 mm wetsuit suits most divers; we carry 5 mm if you run cold, at no extra cost.

What is a halocline?

Fresh rainwater floating on heavier salt water. The boundary between them refracts light, so swimming through it looks like the water has turned to oil or frosted glass — then it closes behind you and the view snaps back. It is one of the strangest things you will ever see underwater.

When is the best time of year to come?

Cenotes are sheltered from wind and swell, so they dive well all year. If you are chasing the light beams, aim for 11:00–13:00 between March and September. Reef conditions are best from March to June.

How many divers will be in my group?

Four, maximum, per guide — in the caverns that is a safety decision as much as a comfort one. Courses run with two students.
